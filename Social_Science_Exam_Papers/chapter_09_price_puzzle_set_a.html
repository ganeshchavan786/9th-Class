OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & STEP-BY-STEP EXPLANATIONS (SET A)
Q. No. Correct Curricular Reference & Conceptual Rationale
Q.1 (A) A market is any framework (physical, electronic, or telephone) that facilitates exchange between buyers and sellers.
Q.2 (A) The Law of Demand states that higher prices lead to lower quantity demanded, and lower prices lead to higher quantity demanded.
Q.3 (A) The demand curve slopes downwards from left to right, reflecting the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.
Q.4 (A) The Law of Supply states that higher prices incentivize producers to supply larger quantities to maximize profits, yielding an upward-sloping supply curve.
Q.5 (A) The supply curve slopes upwards from left to right because higher prices encourage sellers to increase output.
Q.6 (A) Market equilibrium occurs where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied at a mutually agreed price.
Q.7 (A) The equilibrium price balances quantity demanded and supplied, clearing the market without shortages or surpluses.
Q.8 (A) At a price above equilibrium, producers want to sell more than consumers want to buy, creating an unsold surplus.
Q.9 (A) Sellers competing to unload excess inventory discount their prices, which stimulates buying and contracts supply until equilibrium is restored.
Q.10 (A) When price is below equilibrium, goods are cheap and desired in large quantities, but producers supply less, resulting in a shortage.
Q.11 (A) Frustrated buyers offer higher prices, signalling producers to expand output, which rations demand until equilibrium is reached.
Q.12 (A) Substitutes can replace one another; an increase in the price of tea typically increases the demand for coffee.
Q.13 (A) Because tea and coffee are substitutes, consumers switch to relatively cheaper coffee, shifting its demand curve to the right.
Q.14 (A) Complementary goods are used together; a rise in the price of petrol reduces the demand for cars.
Q.15 (A) Higher petrol operating costs reduce the overall willingness of consumers to purchase petrol cars.
Q.16 (A) For normal goods, higher household purchasing power shifts the demand curve to the right as people consume more.
Q.17 (A) Cost-saving technology reduces unit production expenses, encouraging producers to supply more at each price level (rightward supply shift).
Q.18 (A) A crop failure contracts supply (shift left), leading to shortages that push the equilibrium price sharply upwards.
Q.19 (A) A price ceiling sets a legal maximum price to protect low-income consumers, often set below equilibrium on essentials like medicines.
Q.20 (A) A price floor (like MSP) sets a minimum price above equilibrium to guarantee agricultural producers fair returns against market crashes.
Q.21 (A) Price elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
Q.22 (A) Necessities lack close substitutes; consumers must purchase them regardless of price hikes, making demand price-inelastic.
Q.23 (A) Under perfect competition, numerous small buyers and sellers trade standardized goods, so everyone is a price-taker.
Q.24 (A) A monopoly exists when a single producer dominates the market and exercises pricing power due to high barriers to entry.
Q.25 (A) Adam Smith described the 'Invisible Hand' of market prices, where individual self-interest unwittingly guides resources to their highest-valued social use.
