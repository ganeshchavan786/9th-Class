SECTION A: CORE THEORETICAL PRINCIPLES (Q.1 TO Q.10) [10 MARKS]
Q.1

Assertion (A): Palaeolithic hunter-gatherers lived a highly mobile nomadic lifestyle rather than settling permanently.

Reason (R): They depended on naturally available seasonal wild plants and migratory wild game animals that fluctuated across geographic zones.

Q.2

Assertion (A): The Mesolithic period is distinguished technologically by the widespread manufacture and use of microliths.

Reason (R): Microliths are minute flint or chert stone tools, often hafted onto bone or wooden handles to construct composite tools like sickles and spears.

Q.3

Assertion (A): The Neolithic revolution marked a fundamental transition from food gathering to food production.

Reason (R): Humans began domesticating plants (wheat, barley, rice) and animals (sheep, goats, cattle), leading to sedentary village communities.

Q.4

Assertion (A): Pottery making became essential during the Neolithic period rather than the early Palaeolithic.

Reason (R): The production of agricultural surpluses required durable storage vessels to store grains and liquid dairy products and cook cereal foods.

Q.5

Assertion (A): Bhimbetka rock shelters in Madhya Pradesh contain prehistoric paintings depicting only modern industrial machines.

Reason (R): Bhimbetka cave art was created by Upper Palaeolithic and Mesolithic hunter-gatherers depicting wild animals, hunting expeditions, and community rituals.

Q.6

Assertion (A): The Harappan Civilisation is classified as an Iron Age civilization.

Reason (R): Harappan artisans possessed advanced metallurgical knowledge of copper and bronze but did not possess iron technology.

Q.7

Assertion (A): Mehrgarh in Baluchistan provides one of the earliest archaeological evidences of farming and pastoralism in South Asia.

Reason (R): Excavations at Mehrgarh reveal sequential strata documenting the transition from hunting wild game to cultivating domesticated barley and herding cattle.

Q.8

Assertion (A): Burnt clay bricks used in Harappan cities had standard uniform dimensional proportions of 1:2:4 (thickness : width : length).

Reason (R): Standardized brick ratios across disparate settlements from Gujarat to Punjab reflect centralized civic planning and technological uniformity.

Q.9

Assertion (A): Polished stone axes (celts) with ground cutting edges are diagnostic artifacts of the Lower Palaeolithic era.

Reason (R): Neolithic toolmakers ground and polished stone axes on gritty sandstone to produce sharper, more resilient cutting edges for clearing forests.

Q.10

Assertion (A): Harappan settlements were typically divided into two distinct sectors: a western Citadel and an eastern Lower Town.

Reason (R): The elevated Citadel contained monumental public buildings and elite structures, while the Lower Town was planned for residential housing.

SECTION B: EVIDENTIARY PROOFS & MECHANISMS (Q.11 TO Q.18) [8 MARKS]
Q.11

Assertion (A): The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro was constructed using watertight bitumen mortar.

Reason (R): Builders lined the brickwork with a layer of natural bitumen (tar) and gypsum plaster to prevent subterranean water leakage.

Q.12

Assertion (A): Harappan cities lacked any organized drainage systems, leaving sewage to overflow openly in the streets.

Reason (R): Harappan street drains were meticulously covered with brick slabs, equipped with inspection sumps, and connected directly to household bathing platforms.

Q.13

Assertion (A): Lothal in Gujarat served as an important maritime port and trade hub for the Harappan Civilisation.

Reason (R): Archaeologists excavated a massive tidal brick basin at Lothal designed to dock marine ships arriving from the Gulf of Khambhat.

Q.14

Assertion (A): Harappan craft production was concentrated exclusively within small rural households without any regional specialization.

Reason (R): Specialized craft workshops at Chanhudaro and Lothal produced micro-beads, shell bangles, and etched carnelian beads for long-distance export.

Q.15

Assertion (A): The famous bronze figurine known as the 'Dancing Girl' from Mohenjo-daro was manufactured using the lost-wax (cire perdue) casting technique.

Reason (R): Lost-wax casting involves creating a beeswax model, coating it with clay, melting out the wax, and pouring molten metal into the hollow cavity.

Q.16

Assertion (A): Harappan seals made of steatite typically carried intaglio carvings of animals alongside pictographic inscriptions.

Reason (R): Seals were stamped onto wet clay tags affixed to bundles of trade merchandise to verify authenticity and indicate source of origin.

Q.17

Assertion (A): Dholavira in the Rann of Kutch is renowned for its sophisticated rock-cut water reservoirs and hydraulic management system.

Reason (R): In an arid environment with seasonal streams, Dholavira engineers constructed dams across the Manhar and Mansar channels to divert rainwater into massive reservoirs.

Q.18

Assertion (A): Harappan trade was strictly confined within the boundaries of the Indus valley without any external foreign trade contacts.

Reason (R): Harappan artifacts, including etched carnelian beads and Indus seals, have been recovered from ancient Mesopotamian sites like Ur and Kish.

SECTION C: ADVANCED NUANCES & INSTITUTIONAL COMPLEXITIES (Q.19 TO Q.25) [7 MARKS]
Q.19

Assertion (A): The Harappan script has been successfully deciphered by modern epigraphists using bilingual trilingual inscriptions.

Reason (R): No bilingual inscription like the Rosetta Stone has ever been discovered for the Harappan script, and its inscriptions remain brief and undeciphered.

Q.20

Assertion (A): Harappan metrology employed a dual binary and decimal weighing system.

Reason (R): Smaller weights for precious stones followed a binary progression (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64), while larger weights followed a decimal system.

Q.21

Assertion (A): The decline of mature Harappan urbanism around 1900 BCE is attributed to a single cataclysmic foreign military conquest.

Reason (R): Archaeological evidence points to a combination of climatic drying, tectonic shifts altering river courses (such as the Ghaggar-Hakra), and trade breakdown.

Q.22

Assertion (A): The 'Pashupati' seal from Mohenjo-daro depicts a seated three-faced figure surrounded by an elephant, tiger, rhinoceros, and buffalo.

Reason (R): Sir John Marshall interpreted this figure as an early proto-Shiva representation due to the yogic posture, horned headdress, and association with wild beasts.

Q.23

Assertion (A): Harappan burial customs demonstrate that they placed copper tools, pottery vessels, and personal ornaments alongside the deceased.

Reason (R): The inclusion of everyday provisions in graves suggests a belief in life after death or an afterlife where the deceased would require material goods.

Q.24

Assertion (A): Kalibangan in Rajasthan has yielded evidence of furrowed agricultural fields and clay fire altars.

Reason (R): The ploughed field at Kalibangan shows intersecting furrows suggesting two different crops (mustard and gram) were grown simultaneously.

Q.25

Assertion (A): The transition from Mature Harappan to Late Harappan culture witnessed an expansion of monumental palaces and centralized municipal governance.

Reason (R): The Late Harappan phase was characterized by de-urbanization, abandonment of large cities, loss of the standardized script, and a shift to localized rural settlements.
