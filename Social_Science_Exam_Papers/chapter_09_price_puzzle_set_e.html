CASE STUDY 1: Supply Shocks, Inelasticity & the Onion Price Rollercoaster

The market for onions in India is notoriously vulnerable to extreme price volatility. In September 2023, unseasonal torrential rains and flash floods in Nashik, Maharashtra (the primary onion-producing hub of India), severely damaged the standing Kharif crop and submerged storage warehouses. Agricultural economists analyzed the market using demand and supply curves. Because onions are an essential dietary staple in Indian households with virtually no direct culinary substitutes, the market demand curve for onions is steep and highly price-inelastic ($E_d < 1$). When the localized flooding occurred, the market supply curve shifted sharply to the left (from $S_0$ to $S_1$). At the prevailing original equilibrium price of ₹25 per kg, a massive market shortage (excess demand) emerged. Because consumers were desperate to buy essential cooking ingredients and could not easily postpone purchases, desperate buyers bid prices up aggressively. Retail prices quadrupled to ₹100 per kg in major cities. Compounding the crisis, speculative wholesale traders began hoarding existing warehouse stocks, anticipating further price rises, which shifted immediate market supply even further leftward until the central government imposed export bans and released buffer stocks.
Q.1 Why did the equilibrium retail price of onions surge from ₹25 to ₹100 per kg following unseasonal rains in Nashik?
Q.2 Why is the price elasticity of demand for essential commodities like onions and common salt classified as 'inelastic' ($E_d < 1$)?
Q.3 What happens in a competitive market when the current price is temporarily below the equilibrium price?
Q.4 How does speculative hoarding by wholesale traders aggravate an existing agricultural supply crisis?
Q.5 What policy intervention did the government deploy to curb the surging domestic retail price of onions?

CASE STUDY 2: Market Equilibrium & Price Discovery in the Urban Poultry Market

In an urban wholesale market in Pune, daily trading in farm-fresh eggs establishes market equilibrium through the interactions of competitive buyers and sellers. The market demand schedule reflects the Law of Demand: at ₹10 per egg, daily quantity demanded is 10,000 eggs; at ₹7 per egg, quantity demanded rises to 25,000 eggs; and at ₹4 per egg, quantity demanded expands to 40,000 eggs. Conversely, the market supply schedule reflects the Law of Supply: at ₹10 per egg, poultry farms are willing to supply 40,000 eggs (attracted by high profit margins); at ₹7 per egg, they supply 25,000 eggs; and at ₹4 per egg, they supply only 10,000 eggs. Market equilibrium is established at the intersection point where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied ($Q_d = Q_s = 25,000$ eggs) at the market-clearing equilibrium price of ₹7 per egg. If sellers attempt to charge ₹10, quantity supplied (40,000) exceeds quantity demanded (10,000), leaving an unsold surplus of 30,000 eggs. To prevent spoilage, competing sellers slash prices downward toward ₹7. If price drops to ₹4, a shortage of 30,000 eggs bids prices back upward to ₹7.
Q.6 Based on the provided market data, what is the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity in the Pune egg market?
Q.7 What market condition develops if poultry sellers attempt to hold the price artificially at ₹10 per egg?
Q.8 Why does a market surplus force competitive sellers to reduce their prices downward toward equilibrium?
Q.9 According to the Law of Supply, why does quantity supplied increase when market prices rise?
Q.10 What term describes the price at which quantity demanded exactly equals quantity supplied, clearing the market?

CASE STUDY 3: Cross-Price Elasticity: Electric Vehicles (EVs) vs Petrol Cars & Subsidies

The automotive industry in India provides a compelling case study in cross-price relationships, substitute goods, and government fiscal interventions. Electric Vehicles (EVs) and conventional Internal Combustion Engine (ICE) petrol vehicles are classified by economists as 'Substitute Goods'—commodities that satisfy the same fundamental consumer transportation want. When international crude oil prices spiked, retail petrol prices crossed ₹105 per litre. Because petrol and ICE cars are 'Complementary Goods' (used together in joint demand), surging petrol running costs caused the demand curve for ICE petrol cars to shift leftward. Simultaneously, the cross-price effect boosted the demand for substitute electric vehicles, shifting the EV demand curve to the right. To accelerate this green transition, the central government launched the FAME-II scheme (Faster Adoption and Manufacturing of Electric Vehicles), granting direct production subsidies and battery incentives to EV manufacturers. Subsidies reduced per-unit marginal manufacturing costs, shifting the market supply curve for EVs rightward (from $S_0$ to $S_1$), lowering the equilibrium retail price of EVs and expanding equilibrium sales.

Q.12 Why does a sharp increase in the retail price of petrol cause the demand curve for petrol cars to shift to the left?
Q.13 How does a government production subsidy provided to EV manufacturers affect the market supply curve for electric vehicles?

CASE STUDY 4: Statutory Price Ceilings & the Public Distribution System (PDS)

In free competitive markets, equilibrium prices are determined purely by interaction of supply and demand without regard to consumer poverty or social equity. For essential survival commodities like wheat, rice, and life-saving antibiotics, the free-market equilibrium price ($P^*$) may be so high that millions of low-income households are priced out and face starvation. To protect vulnerable citizens, the government intervenes by imposing a legally binding 'Price Ceiling'—a statutory maximum legal price set strictly below the free-market equilibrium level ($P_c < P^*$). While well-intentioned, economic analysis demonstrates the inevitable market consequence: at the artificially low ceiling price $P_c$, quantity demanded ($Q_d$) expands substantially, while profit-seeking producers contract quantity supplied ($Q_s$). This creates an endemic market shortage (excess demand). Without government intervention, shortages lead to black marketing and long queues. To ensure equitable delivery, the Indian state operates the Targeted Public Distribution System (PDS), distributing subsidized food grains via fair price shops to ration card holders, backed by direct budgetary food subsidies.
Q.16 What is a 'Price Ceiling' in economic policy, and where is it set relative to the free-market equilibrium price?
Q.17 What inevitable market disequilibrium is generated when a price ceiling is enforced below the equilibrium price?
Q.18 Why does the government operate Fair Price Shops under the Public Distribution System (PDS) in conjunction with price ceilings?
Q.19 If an unregulated free market experiences an acute shortage of a life-saving medicine, what happens to its price?
Q.20 How does the government finance the gap between the economic cost of procuring food grains and the subsidized PDS issue price?

CASE STUDY 5: Agricultural Price Floors: Minimum Support Price (MSP) & Buffer Stocks

Agricultural production in India is subject to extreme seasonal supply gluts. During post-harvest months, millions of farmers harvest crops simultaneously. Because market supply temporarily swamps demand, open-market prices can plummet below the actual cost of cultivation, driving farmers into debt traps and distress sales. To stabilize farm incomes and ensure national food security, the Government of India announces a 'Minimum Support Price' (MSP) for 22 mandated crops prior to each sowing season, based on recommendations by the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP). Economically, the MSP operates as a 'Price Floor'—a statutory minimum price fixed above the free-market equilibrium level ($P_f > P^*$). At this elevated price floor, quantity supplied exceeds open commercial demand, creating an unsold market surplus. To make the price floor effective and prevent market collapse, public procurement agencies, primarily the Food Corporation of India (FCI), step into agricultural mandis and purchase all surplus food grains offered by farmers at the announced MSP. These procured grains are stored in national buffer stocks, which supply the PDS and serve as strategic reserves during drought emergencies.
Q.21 What is the primary economic objective of the Minimum Support Price (MSP) policy in Indian agriculture?
Q.22 Economically, where is a Minimum Support Price (Price Floor) fixed relative to the free-market equilibrium price?
Q.23 What market condition inevitably emerges when a price floor is fixed above the equilibrium price?
Q.24 What agency purchases surplus food grains at the MSP to prevent open-market prices from collapsing below the floor?
Q.25 What double purpose do the buffer stocks of food grains maintained by the Food Corporation of India serve?
