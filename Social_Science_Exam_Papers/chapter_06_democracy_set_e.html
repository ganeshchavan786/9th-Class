CASE STUDY 1: Electoral Manipulation vs Free Contest: Mexico under the PRI (1930–2000)

Between 1930 and 2000, Mexico held national presidential elections every six years without military interruption. On paper, the country maintained a functioning constitutional republic with multi-party competition. Yet, in reality, every single election was won by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). Opposition parties contested regularly, but the playing field was structurally rigged. Civil servants and government employees were forced to attend PRI campaign rallies. Primary school teachers were instructed to coerce parents of school children into voting for PRI candidates. National media, television channels, and newspapers heavily favored the ruling party while ignoring or smearing opposition speeches. Furthermore, in the final hours before polling, the government frequently shifted polling booth locations to remote areas without prior notice, disorienting voters. The PRI spent astronomical sums of state public money to secure its candidates' victories. While elections occurred with procedural regularity, the political system failed the fundamental democratic test: elections were neither free nor fair, offering citizens no genuine opportunity to unseat the ruling regime.
Q.1 Why could Mexico between 1930 and 2000 NOT be classified as a genuine democracy, despite holding regular multi-party elections?
Q.2 What democratic feature is violated when public sector employees and teachers are compelled to campaign for the ruling party?
Q.3 How did shifting polling booth locations at the last minute benefit the ruling PRI in Mexico?
Q.4 What essential condition must be met for an election to be considered 'free and fair'?
Q.5 Which year finally marked the end of the PRI's seven-decade electoral monopoly in Mexico?

CASE STUDY 2: Military Overthrow & Subversion of Democracy: Pakistan under General Musharraf

In October 1999, General Pervez Musharraf led a military coup d'état in Pakistan, overthrowing the democratically elected government of Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif. Musharraf designated himself the 'Chief Executive' and later assumed the presidency. In 2002, he conducted a national referendum granting himself a five-year presidential extension. Pakistani human rights organizations, independent jurists, and opposition parties condemned the referendum as heavily rigged and fraudulent. In August 2002, Musharraf issued the 'Legal Framework Order' (LFO), unilaterally amending the Constitution of Pakistan. Under the LFO, the military President was granted sweeping powers to dismiss the National Assembly and provincial legislatures at will. The work of the civilian cabinet was subordinated to a military-dominated National Security Council. Although parliamentary elections were subsequently held and elected ministers met in Islamabad, sovereign decision-making power rested entirely with military generals and President Musharraf, failing the cardinal test of democracy: sovereign authority must rest with elected representatives.
Q.6 Why could Pakistan under General Musharraf after 2002 NOT be called a genuine democracy, even though an elected parliament existed?
Q.7 What power was granted to the President under the controversial Legal Framework Order (LFO) of 2002 in Pakistan?
Q.8 What body was established under the LFO to supervise the work of civilian ministers?
Q.9 What fundamental principle of democracy was undermined by Musharraf's 2002 referendum and Legal Framework Order?
Q.10 How does a military dictatorship fundamentally differ from a constitutional democracy?

CASE STUDY 3: Political Equality & the Struggle for 'One Person, One Vote, One Value'

Democracy is fundamentally anchored in the principle of political equality. In a genuine democracy, each adult citizen must have one vote, and each vote must carry equal weight ('one person, one vote, one value'). Throughout global history, however, this principle has faced structural resistance and discrimination. In Saudi Arabia, women were completely denied the right to vote until municipal elections in 2015. In Estonia, citizenship and language laws enacted after the collapse of the Soviet Union made it exceptionally difficult for the ethnic Russian minority (comprising over 25% of the population) to acquire citizenship and participate in national elections. In the Pacific island nation of Fiji, the post-coup electoral system was designed so that the vote of an indigenous Fijian carried significantly more mathematical weight than the vote of an Indo-Fijian citizen. Such weighted voting schemes violate the core egalitarian principle of democratic citizenship, treating citizens as unequal based on gender, ethnicity, or linguistic identity.

Q.12 How did Fiji's pre-reform electoral system violate the democratic principle of equal voting value?
Q.13 What obstacle did the ethnic Russian minority face in Estonia's political system after 1991?
Q.14 Until the year 2015, which large demographic group was entirely excluded from exercising the right to vote in Saudi Arabia?
Q.15 Why is political equality essential for the preservation of human dignity in a democracy?

CASE STUDY 4: Democratic Accountability & Famine Prevention: Amartya Sen's Thesis

Nobel laureate economist Amartya Sen conducted groundbreaking comparative research analyzing the relationship between political democracy and famine prevention. Between 1958 and 1961, China suffered the worst recorded famine in human history, known as the Great Chinese Famine during the 'Great Leap Forward', resulting in an estimated 30 million deaths. During the exact same historical era, newly independent India faced comparable agricultural droughts and food grain deficits, yet India never experienced a mass famine of remotely similar scale. Sen explained this disparity through democratic institutional accountability: in China, the totalitarian Communist regime had banned all opposition political parties, censored the press, and silenced independent whistleblowers. Local officials, terrified of punishment, sent falsified, exaggerated crop yield reports to Beijing, and top leaders remained blind to mass starvation. In contrast, democratic India possessed an independent press that immediately reported localized grain shortages, vocal opposition parties that fiercely criticized government neglect in parliament, and ruling politicians who faced swift electoral defeat if they failed to organize emergency food relief. As Sen famously observed: 'No substantial famine has ever occurred in any independent and democratic country with a relatively free press.'

CASE STUDY 5: Constitutional Limits on Power & Rule of Law: Zimbabwe under Robert Mugabe

Zimbabwe gained national independence from white minority rule in 1980. The country was subsequently led by Robert Mugabe, a charismatic leader of the freedom struggle, and his party ZANU-PF. Zimbabwe held regular presidential and parliamentary elections, and Mugabe consistently won reelection. Yet, political analysts agree that Zimbabwe ceased to function as a genuine constitutional democracy. Over three decades, President Mugabe amended the national constitution repeatedly to expand executive presidential powers and diminish executive accountability. Opposition party workers from the Movement for Democratic Change (MDC) were systematically harassed, beaten, and their meetings violently disrupted. The government controlled state television and radio networks, broadcasting exclusively ruling-party propaganda, while independent journalists were jailed under draconian defamation laws. Crucially, Mugabe's regime openly defied court rulings issued by independent judges and pressured judges to resign. Zimbabwe proved that popular electoral approval alone does not guarantee democracy; a true democracy requires institutional rule of law, separation of powers, and constitutional respect for basic civil rights.

Q.23 How does constitutional democracy protect against the 'tyranny of the majority'?
Q.24 What key distinction exists between a 'popular' government and a 'democratic' government?
