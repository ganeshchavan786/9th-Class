OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & STEP-BY-STEP EXPLANATIONS (SET B)
Q. No. Correct Curricular Reference & Analytical Rationale
Q.1 (A) Falling prices induce consumers to substitute away from expensive alternatives (substitution effect) and enhance real purchasing power (income effect), while diminishing marginal utility lowers reservation prices.
Q.2 (A) Own-price changes cause movements along the demand curve ($P \leftrightarrow Q$); shifts in income, preferences, or substitute prices shift the entire schedule left or right.
Q.3 (A) Substitutes have positive cross-price elasticity: rising operating costs for petrol vehicles drive consumers toward electric alternatives, shifting electric scooter demand right.
Q.4 (A) A binding price ceiling ($P_{cap} < P_{eq}$) creates excess demand (shortage) and disincentivizes manufacturers, breeding illicit black markets and rationing queues.
Q.5 (A) A binding price floor ($P_{MSP} > P_{eq}$) induces farmers to produce more than consumers buy at that price, resulting in a surplus that state agencies (FCI) must buy.
Q.6 (A) Necessities lacking close substitutes exhibit price-inelastic demand: consumers must buy them to survive, showing low sensitivity to price fluctuations.
Q.7 (A) Cost-reducing technology shifts the marginal cost curve down and market supply right ($S_1 \rightarrow S_2$), driving down equilibrium price and expanding adoption.
Q.8 (A) Complements exhibit negative cross-price elasticity: costlier petrol raises the total cost of car ownership, suppressing demand for cars (leftward shift).
Q.9 (A) Consumer surplus is the area beneath the demand curve and above the market price, representing the net psychological and economic welfare gained by consumers.
Q.10 (A) Producer surplus is the area above the supply curve and below the equilibrium price line, reflecting producer revenue over marginal production costs.
Q.11 (A) Both forces exert upward pressure on price (higher demand bids price up; restricted supply drives price up); quantity effects oppose each other and depend on shift magnitudes.
Q.12 (A) Inferior goods possess negative income elasticity ($E_Y < 0$): rising household purchasing power allows consumers to upgrade to superior goods (e.g., from coarse grain to basmati rice).
Q.13 (A) Monopolistic competition blends competition (many sellers, free entry) with monopoly power over brand differentiation (advertising, packaging, distinct features).
Q.14 (A) The invisible hand of the price mechanism rations scarce goods: rising prices incentivize supply expansion while trimming marginal demand back to balance.
Q.15 (A) Natural monopolies exhibit immense economies of scale across the relevant market range; duplicating water mains or power lines across streets would be wastefully duplicative.
Q.16 (A) Predatory pricing is an anti-competitive practice: taking short-term losses to extinguish competition, followed by monopolistic price gouging once market entry barriers rise.
Q.17 (A) Perfect elasticity ($E_s = \infty$) is represented by a horizontal line: producers will supply infinite quantity at that price, but zero quantity below it.
Q.18 (A) Inelastic demand experiences minimal quantity contraction when taxed, minimizing deadweight loss and yielding dependable excise revenues for public health programs.
Q.19 (A) Deadweight loss represents allocative inefficiency: transactions that would have generated mutual welfare benefits are prevented by price wedges or quotas.
Q.20 (A) Persuasive marketing enhances perceived differentiation, shifting consumer demand rightward and reducing brand sensitivity to rival price discounts.
Q.21 (A) Warehousing and futures contracts facilitate inter-temporal price smoothing: storing post-harvest gluts for release during lean months protects farmer margins and consumer prices.
Q.22 (A) Cartels suffer the prisoner's dilemma: each member has an incentive to covertly overproduce, and elevated prices incentivize renewable energy and non-cartel drilling.
Q.23 (A) Conspicuous consumption goods derive utility from exclusivity: high prices signal social status, leading prestige-seeking consumers to purchase more at higher prices.
Q.24 (A) In competitive markets, positive economic profits attract new entrants; the resulting supply expansion ($S \rightarrow S'$) depresses prices until firms earn normal profit.
Q.25 (A) Adam Smith showed that decentralized price signals harmonize individual profit-seeking with social resource allocation, without requiring an omnipresent central planner.
