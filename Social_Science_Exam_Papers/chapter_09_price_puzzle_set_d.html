SECTION A: CORE THEORETICAL PRINCIPLES (Q.1 TO Q.10) [10 MARKS]
Q.1

Assertion (A): According to the Law of Demand, there is an inverse relationship between the price of a commodity and its quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.

Reason (R): When the price of a commodity falls, consumers experience an increase in purchasing power (income effect) and substitute it for costlier alternatives (substitution effect).

Q.2

Assertion (A): A market demand curve slopes upward from left to right.

Reason (R): Higher market prices incentivize consumers to purchase significantly larger quantities of a good.

Q.3

Assertion (A): According to the Law of Supply, a producer offers more quantity for sale at a higher price than at a lower price, other things being equal.

Reason (R): Higher commodity prices increase the profit margin per unit, motivating sellers to expand output and attracting new producers into the market.

Q.4

Assertion (A): Market equilibrium occurs at the price where quantity demanded exactly equals quantity supplied.

Reason (R): At the equilibrium price, there is neither an unsold market surplus nor an unsatisfied market shortage, clearing the market.

Q.5

Assertion (A): If the prevailing market price is higher than the equilibrium price, an excess demand (shortage) develops in the market.

Reason (R): At a price above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating an excess supply (surplus) that forces sellers to cut prices.

Q.6

Assertion (A): When a severe drought destroys major onion crops, the market equilibrium price of onions rises sharply.

Reason (R): A crop failure causes the supply curve of onions to shift to the left, creating excess demand at the original price and bidding up market prices.

Q.7

Assertion (A): A change in the own price of a good leads to a shift in its demand curve.

Reason (R): A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the same demand curve (expansion or contraction), while non-price factors cause a shift.

Q.8

Assertion (A): An increase in consumer income leads to a rightward shift in the demand curve for normal goods.

Reason (R): With higher income, consumers are able and willing to purchase larger quantities of normal goods at every given price level.

Q.9

Assertion (A): Tea and coffee are classified as substitute goods in economic analysis.

Reason (R): If the price of tea rises substantially, consumers switch to coffee, causing the demand for coffee to increase and its demand curve to shift rightward.

Q.10

Assertion (A): Cars and petrol are classified as complementary goods.

Reason (R): A sharp rise in the price of petrol increases consumer operational costs, causing a decline in the demand for petrol-driven cars.

SECTION B: EVIDENTIARY PROOFS & MECHANISMS (Q.11 TO Q.18) [8 MARKS]
Q.11

Assertion (A): Essential commodities like common salt and life-saving medicines have highly price-inelastic demand.

Reason (R): Consumers must consume fixed, necessary quantities of these items regardless of price fluctuations because they have no close substitutes.

Q.12

Assertion (A): Luxury commodities such as designer jewellery and sports cars tend to have highly price-elastic demand.

Reason (R): Purchases of luxury items can be easily postponed or avoided when their prices rise, as they do not constitute basic physical survival needs.

Q.13

Assertion (A): Technological improvements in manufacturing reduce the per-unit production cost and shift the market supply curve to the right.

Reason (R): Lower production costs allow firms to supply larger quantities of output at every existing market price, leading to a lower equilibrium price.

Q.14

Assertion (A): Imposition of a per-unit excise tax on manufacturers shifts the supply curve vertically upwards to the left.

Reason (R): Taxes increase the effective marginal cost of production, forcing sellers to demand a higher price to supply the same quantity.

Q.15

Assertion (A): A government-mandated price ceiling is set below the free-market equilibrium price.

Reason (R): Price ceilings are imposed on essential goods (such as staple food grains or medicines) to make them affordable to low-income vulnerable citizens.

Q.16

Assertion (A): A legally enforced price ceiling set below equilibrium eliminates all shortages and black markets.

Reason (R): At an artificially low price ceiling, quantity demanded vastly exceeds quantity supplied, creating chronic shortages that necessitate rationing.

Q.17

Assertion (A): A Minimum Support Price (MSP) for agricultural crops acts as a price floor set above the free-market equilibrium price.

Reason (R): MSP guarantees farmers a fair remunerative price that covers their comprehensive cost of cultivation and protects them from distress sales during harvest gluts.

Q.18

Assertion (A): When the government fixes an MSP above equilibrium price, it creates an unsold agricultural surplus in the open market.

Reason (R): To sustain the MSP and prevent market price collapse, public procurement agencies like the Food Corporation of India (FCI) purchase the surplus grain for buffer stocks.

SECTION C: ADVANCED NUANCES & INSTITUTIONAL COMPLEXITIES (Q.19 TO Q.25) [7 MARKS]
Q.19

Assertion (A): Giffen goods violate the general Law of Demand by exhibiting a positive relationship between price and quantity demanded.

Reason (R): For highly inferior staple food grains consumed by impoverished households, the negative income effect of a price rise overwhelms the substitution effect.

Q.20

Assertion (A): Veblen goods (conspicuous consumption goods) are demanded in larger quantities when their prices become extremely low and cheap.

Reason (R): Veblen goods derive their social value from their exclusivity and high price status; if they become cheap, their snob appeal diminishes.

Q.21

Assertion (A): A perfectly inelastic demand curve is represented graphically as a vertical straight line parallel to the vertical price axis.

Reason (R): With perfectly inelastic demand, quantity demanded remains completely unchanged ($E_d = 0$) regardless of how high or low the price moves.

Q.22

Assertion (A): A simultaneous increase in both demand and supply always causes the equilibrium price to increase.

Reason (R): Whether the equilibrium price rises, falls, or remains constant depends on the relative magnitude of the shift in demand compared to the shift in supply.

Q.23

Assertion (A): Speculative hoarding by traders during anticipated shortages worsens the price rise in the market.

Reason (R): Hoarding artificially withdraws available supplies from current circulation, shifting the immediate supply curve further leftward and accelerating inflation.

Q.24

Assertion (A): The Public Distribution System (PDS) in India operates fair price shops to supply subsidized essential food grains to ration-card holders.

Reason (R): PDS buffers vulnerable households against volatile open-market inflationary spikes and ensures national food security.

Q.25

Assertion (A): In a free competitive market economy, the price mechanism automatically allocates scarce resources without a central planning ministry.

Reason (R): Prices act as market signals: high prices signal consumer demand and profitability, guiding producers to allocate more resources to those sectors.
