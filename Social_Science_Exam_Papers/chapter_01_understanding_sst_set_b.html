OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & STEP-BY-STEP EXPLANATIONS (SET B)
Q. No. Correct Curricular Reference & Analytical Rationale
Q.1 (A) Analytical history goes beyond 'what happened when' to evaluate causality, socio-economic transformations, conflicting sources, and human agency.
Q.2 (A) Historical source criticism requires verifying material authenticity, royal ideological intent, donor-beneficiary motives, and cross-referencing with other epigraphs.
Q.3 (A) Hoards of Roman aurei and denarii confirm extensive Indo-Roman maritime trade in pepper, textiles, and gemstones, with Pliny lamenting the drain of Roman gold to India.
Q.4 (A) Interdisciplinary inquiry synthesizes tools from multiple social science fields to diagnose complex multidimensional societal issues.
Q.5 (A) $^{14}\text{C}$ dating depends on organic carbon uptake during life; non-organic minerals and stones lack metabolic carbon and require relative stratigraphy or thermoluminescence.
Q.6 (A) Identifying that two social trends co-occur (correlation) does not prove that one generated the other (causation), avoiding logical fallacies in policy.
Q.7 (A) Official court histories frequently served ideological legitimacy and imperial self-fashioning, necessitating critical comparison with non-court records.
Q.8 (A) Disparities in grave goods (mortuary analysis) reflect institutionalized social stratification and unequal access to prestige resources in ancient societies.
Q.9 (A) Environmental determinism posits nature as the controlling force, whereas possibilism highlights human technological agency and adaptive choices.
Q.10 (A) Ethical anthropology mandates informed consent, reciprocity, cultural sensitivity, confidentiality, and protection of indigenous intellectual property.
Q.11 (A) Subaltern and oral histories democratize historical inquiry by uncovering the lived experiences of women, peasants, artisans, and marginalized groups.
Q.12 (A) Development economics analyzes the trade-off between macro-level economic growth and localized displacement, equity, and human rights.
Q.13 (A) Presentism introduces anachronistic bias by evaluating historical figures through modern moral paradigms without grasping their contemporary cultural contexts.
Q.14 (A) A wide base indicates high birth rates, while a tapering apex reflects high mortality across older cohorts, typical of developing demographies.
Q.15 (A) Lived psychological experiences, subjective meanings, and social alienation are explored through qualitative interviews and ethnography, not bare numbers.
Q.16 (A) Civic agency views citizens as conscious participants who organize, deliberate, protest, and shape public institutions beyond passive voting.
Q.17 (A) Integrating satellite telemetry with ground truthing ensures multi-scalar precision, validating digital signatures against real landscape features.
Q.18 (A) Culture is fluid, contested, and evolving through dialogue, migration, technological shifts, and internal socio-political contestations.
Q.19 (A) Historiographical debate reflects diverse analytical frameworks (e.g., Marxist, nationalist, imperialist) interpreting factual evidence through different lenses.
Q.20 (A) Separation of powers and reciprocal institutional checks prevent the concentration of arbitrary power, preserving constitutional liberties.
Q.21 (A) Top-down technocratic planning that ignores sociological nuances, caste hierarchies, and customary water norms frequently leads to policy failure.
Q.22 (A) Externalities highlight market failures where private costs diverge from social costs (e.g., air pollution), justifying public regulation and taxes.
Q.23 (A) Reading 'against the grain' requires deconstructing administrative terminology to recover the socio-economic grievances masked by state reports.
Q.24 (A) Spatial epidemiological mapping (as John Snow pioneered in 1854) connects disease transmission vectors with environmental and infrastructural variables.
Q.25 (A) Substantive policy evaluation relies on empirical multidimensional well-being indicators rather than public relations announcements.
