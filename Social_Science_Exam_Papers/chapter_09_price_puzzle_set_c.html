OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & STEP-BY-STEP EXPLANATIONS (SET C)
Q. No. Correct Curricular Reference & In-Depth Conceptual Solution
Q.1 (A) For a Giffen good, when the staple's price rises, poor consumers become so impoverished that they cut out expensive foods (meat) and buy *more* of the cheap staple to survive ($E_p > 0$).
Q.2 (A) A rectangular hyperbola has the mathematical property $P \times Q = \text{Constant}$; total consumer outlay remains unchanged when price changes, meaning elasticity equals 1 everywhere.
Q.3 (A) OMSS regulates market volatility: selling state buffer stocks in wholesale spot auctions expands market supply, curbing speculative hoarding and food price spikes.
Q.4 (A) $E_{xy} = \%\Delta Q_x / \%\Delta P_y < 0$. Complementary goods (printers and ink cartridges) move inversely: costlier ink suppresses printer sales.
Q.5 (A) Price discrimination captures consumer surplus by segmenting markets: charging higher prices to inelastic buyers (business travelers) and lower prices to elastic buyers (students).
Q.6 (A) Tax incidence formula: $\Delta P_c / \Delta P_p = E_s / |E_d|$. Inelastic consumers bear the brunt because they cannot easily substitute away; elastic consumers force producers to absorb the tax.
Q.7 (A) The PSF finances market intervention: procurement of perishable commodities (onions, pulses) during post-harvest price crashes and market release during lean shortages.
Q.8 (A) Agricultural biological gestation periods and perishability mean short-run supply is nearly fixed ($E_s \approx 0$); farmers must sell harvest stock at prevailing prices regardless of market crashes.
Q.9 (A) Oligopoly features strategic interdependence (kinked demand curves, game theory): a price cut by one telecom operator triggers retaliatory price wars from rival carriers.
Q.10 (A) Arbitrageurs buy low in surplus regions and sell high in deficit regions; transport-adjusted arbitrage enforces the 'Law of One Price' across integrated national markets.
Q.11 (A) Non-excludability breeds free-riding: since non-payers cannot be excluded from lighthouse beams or streetlights, private profit-seeking firms cannot collect revenue, necessitating state provision.
Q.12 (A) Information asymmetry leads to adverse selection: buyers offer only average prices because they cannot distinguish quality, incentivizing sellers of high-quality goods to exit.
Q.13 (A) The CCI enforces competition law: breaking up illicit price-fixing cartels, curbing monopolistic predatory behavior, and vetting mergers to safeguard consumer surplus.
Q.14 (A) Lagged supply response ($S_t = f(P_{t-1})$) creates cyclical cobweb oscillations: high prices spur overplanting, resulting in harvest gluts, price collapses, and subsequent shortages.
Q.15 (A) Monopsony is single-buyer dominance: an isolated sugar processing mill exercises buyer power over captive local sugarcane growers who have no other nearby processing outlets.
Q.16 (A) Because the supply of unimproved land is perfectly inelastic ($E_s = 0$), landowners absorb the tax entirely without shifting economic output, creating zero excess burden or deadweight loss.
Q.17 (A) Production substitutes compete for the same productive land: an increase in the relative profitability of soybean diverts acreage away from maize, reducing maize supply.
Q.18 (A) Speculative manias decouple market prices from fundamental discounted cash flows, sustained by self-fulfilling momentum until liquidity contractions pop the bubble.
Q.19 (A) Total Revenue Test: when $|E_d| > 1$, percentage quantity expansion outstrips the percentage price drop, raising revenue; when inelastic, volume gains fail to offset price cuts.
Q.20 (A) Menu costs encompass the friction of repricing: updating digital catalogues, printing price tags, adjusting contracts, and alienating customers, explaining price stickiness.
Q.21 (A) Price caps ($P_{cap} < P_{eq}$) suppress legal supply while stoking demand; frustrated consumers with high reservation prices create lucrative profit margins for illicit black markets.
Q.22 (A) Predatory bidding is anti-competitive: a well-capitalized processor overbids on raw inputs to starve competitors of supply, driving them to insolvency to monopolize output.
Q.23 (A) A vertical demand curve ($E_d = 0$) demonstrates zero price sensitivity: consumers require an exact fixed quantity for physical survival and cannot adjust consumption.
Q.24 (A) The Law of One Price: spatial arbitrage ensures that in efficient, integrated markets, price differentials between two cities cannot exceed the physical cost of transportation.
Q.25 (A) Market equilibrium achieves allocative harmony: marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost ($MSB = MSC$), clearing markets and coordinating complex decentralized human cooperation.
