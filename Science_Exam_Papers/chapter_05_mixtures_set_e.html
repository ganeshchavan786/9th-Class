Case Study 1: Comparative Optical & Filtration Analysis: Solution, Colloid & Suspension

Students were provided with three unlabeled beakers containing mixtures in water:
• Beaker A: 5 g of Copper Sulfate ($CuSO_4$) dissolved in 100 mL of water.
• Beaker B: 2 g of fine Starch powder boiled and cooled in 100 mL of water.
• Beaker C: 5 g of finely crushed Chalk powder stirred in 100 mL of water.
The students conducted three diagnostic practical tests:
1. Tyndall Effect: Directing a red laser beam through each beaker in a dark room.
2. Filtration: Passing each mixture through Whatman No. 1 filter paper.
3. Stability: Leaving the beakers undisturbed for 30 minutes.
Q.1 In which beaker(s) was the luminous path of the laser beam distinctly visible from the side (Tyndall effect)?
Q.2 What was retained on the filter paper after filtering the contents of Beaker C (Chalk and water)?
Q.3 What happened to the three mixtures after standing undisturbed for 30 minutes?
Q.4 How is Beaker B (Starch in water) accurately classified in colloid chemistry?
Q.5 What microscopic kinetic phenomenon keeps colloidal particles in Beaker B from settling over time?

Case Study 3: Paper Chromatography of Synthetic Black Ink

In a forensic chemistry laboratory practical, students analyzed water-soluble black marker ink using ascending paper chromatography.
1. A pencil baseline was drawn 2 cm from the bottom edge of a Whatman chromatography paper strip.

3. The strip was suspended vertically in a chromatography jar containing water as the mobile solvent, with the solvent level 1 cm below the pencil line.
4. As the solvent ascended by capillary action, the single black spot separated into three distinct color bands: Yellow, Red, and Blue.
5. When the solvent front reached 10.0 cm from the baseline, the strip was removed and dried:
- Yellow dye travelled: $3.0\text{ cm}$
- Red dye travelled: $6.0\text{ cm}$
- Blue dye travelled: $8.5\text{ cm}$

Case Study 5: Mixture vs Compound: Heating Iron Filings and Sulfur Powder

Students investigated the difference between a physical mixture and a chemical compound using iron filings and sulfur powder in a mass ratio of $7:4$.

The students tested both samples with a bar magnet, Carbon Disulfide ($CS_2$) solvent, and dilute Hydrochloric Acid ($HCl$).

Q.22 What was observed when Carbon Disulfide ($CS_2$) solvent was added to Sample 1?

Q.24 What gas was evolved when dilute $HCl$ was added to Sample 2 (Compound FeS)?

OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & PRACTICAL INVESTIGATION EXPLANATIONS (SET E)
Q. No. Correct Scientific Rationale & Experimental Data Analysis
Q.1 (B) Colloidal particles in Beaker B (starch sol, 1–100 nm) and suspended particles in Beaker C scatter visible light. True solutions in Beaker A (< 1 nm) do not show Tyndall effect.
Q.2 (B) Chalk particles in a suspension are larger than 100 nm and cannot pass through the microscopic pores of filter paper, leaving solid residue.
Q.3 (A) True solutions (Beaker A) and colloids (Beaker B) are stable and do not settle; suspensions (Beaker C) are unstable and settle under gravity.
Q.4 (B) Starch powder consists of solid macromolecules dispersed in a liquid dispersion medium (water), which defines a sol.
Q.5 (A) Erratic, continuous collisions between colloidal starch particles and rapidly moving water molecules (Brownian motion) counteract gravitational sedimentation.
Q.6 (B) Mass precipitated $= \text{Solubility at 80°C} - \text{Solubility at 20°C} = 55.0\text{ g} - 21.0\text{ g} = 34.0\text{ g}$.
Q.7 (B) Slow, undisturbed cooling allows solute molecules to arrange methodically into a pure crystal lattice, excluding foreign impurities.
Q.8 (B) Evaporation leaves all soluble contaminants behind with the dried product; crystallization purifies the compound by selectively forming pure crystal lattices.
Q.9 (B) $\text{Mass percentage} = \frac{\text{Mass of Solute}}{\text{Mass of Solution}} \times 100 = \frac{21.0}{21.0 + 100.0} \times 100 = \frac{21.0}{121.0} \times 100 \approx 17.36\%$.
Q.10 (B) A solution that temporarily holds more dissolved solute than its thermodynamic saturation equilibrium at that temperature is supersaturated.
Q.11 (A) Graphite is insoluble in water and organic solvents; ink from a pen would dissolve and migrate, ruining the chromatographic baseline.
Q.12 (B) $R_f = \frac{\text{Distance travelled by solute component}}{\text{Distance travelled by solvent front}} = \frac{6.0\text{ cm}}{10.0\text{ cm}} = 0.60$.
Q.13 (C) The Blue dye migrated the farthest ($8.5$ cm, $R_f = 0.85$), indicating greatest solubility in mobile water and weakest affinity for stationary paper fibers.
Q.14 (B) If submerged below the solvent surface, the ink spot simply washes out into the solvent pool instead of migrating up the paper strip.
Q.15 (D) Sand and iron filings are macroscopic insoluble solids separated mechanically using a magnet; chromatography separates molecular solutes.
Q.16 (B) The denser liquid (water, $1.00\text{ g/cm}^3$) sinks to the bottom, while the less dense liquid (kerosene, $0.80\text{ g/cm}^3$) floats on top.
Q.17 (B) Draining the upper liquid through the bottom stopcock would wash residual droplets of the lower liquid into the clean beaker, causing cross-contamination.
Q.18 (A) Shaking volatile liquids builds up internal vapor pressure that can pop the glass stopper; venting releases the pressure safely.
Q.19 (C) Separating funnels are used exclusively for immiscible liquids that do not dissolve in each other and form distinct layers due to density differences.
Q.20 (B) Mustard oil and water are completely immiscible and separate into two distinct layers; ethanol, acetone, and acetic acid are completely miscible with water.
Q.21 (B) In a mixture, iron retains its magnetic properties. In the compound Iron Sulfide (FeS), iron is chemically bonded in a crystal lattice and loses its ferromagnetism.
Q.22 (B) Sulfur is readily soluble in non-polar carbon disulfide ($CS_2$), while iron is insoluble. This is a physical separation method for the mixture.
Q.23 (B) In the mixture, iron reacts independently with dilute acid: $Fe + 2HCl \rightarrow FeCl_2 + H_2 \uparrow$. Hydrogen is odorless and burns with a pop.
Q.24 (B) Iron sulfide reacts chemically: $FeS + 2HCl \rightarrow FeCl_2 + H_2S \uparrow$. Hydrogen sulfide is toxic and smells like rotten eggs.
Q.25 (B) Chemical reaction: $Fe + S \rightarrow FeS + \text{Heat}$. It is irreversible, forms a new stoichiometric compound, and releases significant chemical energy.
