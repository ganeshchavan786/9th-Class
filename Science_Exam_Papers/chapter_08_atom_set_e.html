Case Study 1: Geiger-Marsden Alpha Particle Scattering Experiment

In 1911, under the direction of Ernest Rutherford, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden conducted their landmark alpha particle scattering experiment.
• A radioactive bismuth source emitted a narrow beam of high-energy positively charged alpha ($lpha$) particles ($m \approx 4\text{ u}$, charge $+2e$).
• The $lpha$-beam struck an extremely thin gold foil of thickness approximately $100\text{ nm}$ ($10^{-7}\text{ m}$, about 1000 atoms thick).
• A movable zinc sulfide (ZnS) phosphorescent detector screen and microscope recorded scintillations produced by scattered particles at various angles ($ heta$).
• Observations:
1. Over $99.8\%$ of the $lpha$-particles passed straight through the foil with zero or negligible deflection.
2. A small fraction (about $0.14\%$) were deflected by angles greater than 1°.
3. Only about 1 in 12,000 particles suffered massive deflection (> 90°) or rebounded backwards at nearly 180°.
Q.1 What major atomic structural conclusion did Rutherford deduce from the observation that most $lpha$-particles passed undeflected?
Q.2 Why did Rutherford deduce that the positive charge and nearly all atomic mass reside in a tiny concentrated 'nucleus'?
Q.3 Why was gold chosen as the target foil metal rather than aluminum or iron?
Q.4 Rutherford estimated that the radius of the nucleus is approximately how many orders of magnitude smaller than the radius of the atom?
Q.5 What was the fatal theoretical limitation of Rutherford's planetary model of the atom according to classical physics?

Case Study 2: Cathode Rays, Canal Rays & Subatomic Particle Discovery

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, physicists explored electrical discharge through rarefied gases in evacuated glass discharge tubes (Crookes tubes):
• J.J. Thomson's Cathode Ray Experiment (1897): High voltage ($10\text{ kV}$) applied across electrodes at low pressure ($0.001\text{ mm Hg}$) generated rays travelling from cathode to anode. The rays cast sharp shadows of obstacles, rotated light mica paddle wheels, and deflected towards the positive plate in an electric field. The charge-to-mass ratio was invariant ($e/m = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\text{ C/kg}$).
• E. Goldstein's Canal Ray Experiment (1886): Using a perforated cathode, luminous rays travelled in the opposite direction from anode to cathode, carrying positive charges whose $e/m$ ratio depended on the gas in the tube.

Case Study 3: Bohr-Bury Electronic Configurations & Energy Shell Quantum Levels

To resolve the instability of Rutherford's atomic model, Niels Bohr proposed quantized stationary orbits in 1913. The Bohr-Bury scheme governs the arrangement of electrons in shells (designated K, L, M, N... with principal quantum numbers $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$):
1. Maximum electrons in shell $n = 2n^2$.
2. Maximum electrons in the outermost valence shell = 8 (Octet rule).
3. Electrons do not occupy a new shell until the inner shells are completely filled (stepwise filling).
• Sodium ($Z=11$): Electronic configuration $2, 8, 1$
• Argon ($Z=18$): Electronic configuration $2, 8, 8$
• Potassium ($Z=19$): Electronic configuration $2, 8, 8, 1$
• Calcium ($Z=20$): Electronic configuration $2, 8, 8, 2$

Case Study 4: Valency Dynamics & Chemical Formula Formulation

Students tabulated atomic numbers, electron configurations, valence electrons, and valency for elements from $Z=1$ to $Z=17$ to understand chemical bonding:
• Element P (Lithium, $Z=3$): Config $2, 1 \rightarrow$ Valence electrons = 1 $\rightarrow$ Valency = 1
• Element Q (Nitrogen, $Z=7$): Config $2, 5 \rightarrow$ Valence electrons = 5 $\rightarrow$ Valency = $8 - 5 = 3$
• Element R (Magnesium, $Z=12$): Config $2, 8, 2 \rightarrow$ Valence electrons = 2 $\rightarrow$ Valency = 2
• Element S (Chlorine, $Z=17$): Config $2, 8, 7 \rightarrow$ Valence electrons = 7 $\rightarrow$ Valency = $8 - 7 = 1$

Case Study 5: Isotopes, Isobars & Applications in Medicine and Industry

Mass spectrometry reveals that many chemical elements occur in nature as mixtures of stable isotopes:
• Chlorine: Consists of $^{35}_{17}Cl$ (atomic mass $35\text{ u}$, abundance $75\%$) and $^{37}_{17}Cl$ (atomic mass $37\text{ u}$, abundance $25\%$).
• Hydrogen: Occurs as Protium ($^1_1H$), Deuterium ($^2_1H$), and Tritium ($^3_1H$).
• Isobars: Argon ($^{40}_{18}Ar$) and Calcium ($^{40}_{20}Ca$) possess identical mass numbers ($A=40$) despite having different atomic numbers ($Z=18$ and $Z=20$).
Radioactive isotopes (radioisotopes) find extensive use in medicine, agriculture, and nuclear power.
