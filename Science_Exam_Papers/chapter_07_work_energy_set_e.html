Case Study 1: Conservation of Mechanical Energy in a Roller Coaster Loop

In a physics amusement park dynamics study, a model roller coaster cart of mass $m = 200\text{ kg}$ was analyzed along a smooth vertical track (neglecting friction and air resistance):
• Point A (Starting Hill): Height $h_A = 20.0\text{ m}$ above ground; cart is released from rest ($u_A = 0\text{ m/s}$).
• Point B (Bottom of Loop): Ground level ($h_B = 0.0\text{ m}$).
• Point C (Top of Circular Loop): Height $h_C = 15.0\text{ m}$ above ground.
• Acceleration due to gravity $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.
Q.1 What is the total mechanical energy ($E_{mech} = E_k + E_p$) of the cart at Point A?
Q.2 What is the speed ($v_B$) of the roller coaster cart as it reaches the lowest point, Point B ($h_B = 0$)?
Q.3 What is the gravitational potential energy of the cart at Point C ($h_C = 15.0\text{ m}$)?
Q.4 What is the kinetic energy ($E_{k, C}$) and speed ($v_C$) of the cart at Point C?
Q.5 If track friction dissipated 4,200 J of energy as heat during the descent from A to B, what would the actual kinetic energy at Point B be?

Case Study 2: Lifting vs Dragging: Work Done Against Gravity and Friction

A warehouse worker needs to move a heavy packing crate of mass $m = 60\text{ kg}$ into a delivery truck whose bed is at a vertical height $h = 1.5\text{ m}$ above the ground ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$).
• Method 1 (Direct Vertical Lift): Lifting the crate straight up onto the truck bed.
• Method 2 (Inclined Ramp): Pushing the crate up a smooth wooden ramp of length $L = 5.0\text{ m}$ inclined at an angle $\theta$ (where $\sin\theta = 1.5/5.0 = 0.30$). The friction force resisting motion along the ramp is $f = 80\text{ N}$.
Q.6 What is the minimum work done by the worker against gravity in Method 1 (Direct Vertical Lift)?
Q.7 In Method 2, what is the work done solely to overcome friction ($W_{friction}$) along the 5.0 m ramp?
Q.8 What is the total work done by the worker in pushing the crate up the ramp in Method 2?
Q.9 If Method 2 requires more total energy (1282 J vs 882 J), why does the worker choose to use the ramp?
Q.10 What is the mechanical efficiency ($\eta$) of the inclined ramp as a machine in Method 2?

OFFICIAL ANSWER KEY & PRACTICAL INVESTIGATION EXPLANATIONS (SET E)
Q. No. Correct Scientific Rationale & Experimental Data Analysis
Q.1 (B) $E_{mech} = mgh_A + \frac{1}{2}m u_A^2 = 200\text{ kg} \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 \times 20.0\text{ m} + 0 = 39,200\text{ J}$.
Q.2 (B) By conservation of energy: $\frac{1}{2}m v_B^2 = mgh_A \Rightarrow v_B = \sqrt{2gh_A} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 20.0} = \sqrt{392} \approx 19.8\text{ m/s}$.
Q.3 (A) $E_p = mgh_C = 200 \times 9.8 \times 15.0 = 29,400\text{ J}$.
Q.4 (A) $E_{k, C} = E_{total} - E_{p, C} = 39,200 - 29,400 = 9,800\text{ J}$. Speed: $v_C = \sqrt{\frac{2 E_k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 9800}{200}} = \sqrt{98} \approx 9.9\text{ m/s}$.
Q.5 (B) Actual $E_k = E_{initial} - E_{friction} = 39,200\text{ J} - 4,200\text{ J} = 35,000\text{ J}$.
Q.6 (B) $W_{gravity} = mgh = 60\text{ kg} \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 \times 1.5\text{ m} = 882\text{ J}$.
Q.7 (A) $W_{friction} = f \times L = 80\text{ N} \times 5.0\text{ m} = 400\text{ J}$.
Q.8 (B) $W_{total} = W_{gravity} + W_{friction} = 882\text{ J} + 400\text{ J} = 1282\text{ J}$.
Q.9 (B) Simple machines act as force multipliers: Lifting requires $588\text{ N}$, while pushing up the ramp requires only $F = mg\sin\theta + f = (60 \times 9.8 \times 0.30) + 80 = 176.4 + 80 = 256.4\text{ N}$.
Q.10 (B) $\text{Efficiency} = \frac{\text{Useful Work Output (against gravity)}}{\text{Total Work Input}} \times 100 = \frac{882\text{ J}}{1282\text{ J}} \times 100 \approx 68.8\%$.
Q.11 (B) $W_{out} = mgh = 1000\text{ kg} \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 \times 18.0\text{ m} = 176,400\text{ J}$ (176.4 kJ).
Q.12 (A) $P_{out} = \frac{W_{out}}{t} = \frac{176,400\text{ J}}{300\text{ s}} = 588\text{ W}$.
Q.13 (A) $E_{consumed} = P_{in} \times t = 1000\text{ W} \times 300\text{ s} = 300,000\text{ J} = 300\text{ kJ}$.
Q.14 (B) $\text{Efficiency} = \frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}} \times 100 = \frac{588\text{ W}}{1000\text{ W}} \times 100 = 58.8\%$.
Q.15 (A) Energy in $\text{kWh} = \text{Power (kW)} \times \text{Time (hours)} = 1.0\text{ kW} \times \left(\frac{5}{60}\text{ h}\right) = \frac{1}{12}\text{ kWh} \approx 0.0833\text{ kWh}$.
Q.16 (A) A lever is in rotational equilibrium when the algebraic sum of all moments about the pivot equals zero: $\text{Anticlockwise Moment} = \text{Clockwise Moment}$.
Q.17 (B) $\text{Effort} \times d_E = \text{Load} \times d_L \Rightarrow E \times 50.0 = 3.0 \times 40.0 \Rightarrow E = \frac{120.0}{50.0} = 2.4\text{ N}$.
Q.18 (B) $\text{MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{3.0\text{ N}}{2.4\text{ N}} = 1.25$ (or $\text{MA} = \frac{d_E}{d_L} = \frac{50}{40} = 1.25$).
Q.19 (B) In a wheelbarrow and nutcracker, the load is in the middle (between fulcrum and effort), giving a mechanical advantage always greater than 1.
Q.20 (A) In Class 3 levers, the effort is applied between the fulcrum and load ($d_E < d_L$). While $\text{MA} < 1$ requires more force, it multiplies speed and displacement.
Q.21 (A) In a single fixed pulley, the effort moves the exact same distance as the load ($\text{VR} = 1$). It changes the direction of effort without multiplying force.
Q.22 (B) Pulling downward allows the worker to utilize body weight effectively and ergonomically to raise loads upward.
Q.23 (B) In a 4-pulley system (2 fixed, 2 movable), the load is shared equally among $n = 4$ supporting segments of the continuous rope, making $\text{VR} = 4$.
Q.24 (C) $E = \frac{\text{Load}}{n} = \frac{196\text{ N}}{4} = 49\text{ N}$.
Q.25 (B) Total weight lifted $= \text{Load} + w_{pulley} = 196 + 19.6 = 215.6\text{ N}$. Actual effort $= \frac{215.6}{4} = 53.9\text{ N}$.
